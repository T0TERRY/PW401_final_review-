PW401 Final Review

Complete Guide to Grantseeking & Proposal Writing

📚 Course Overview

PW401 covers the complete grantseeking process from developing fundable project ideas to sustaining relationships with funders.

Key Players in Grantseeking

Lead Program Staff Project Leader who oversees the initiative
Executive Director Provides organizational leadership and approval
Finance Person Manages budget planning and financial tracking
Grantwriting Consultant Expert in proposal development and writing

Grant Seeking Readiness Checklist

  1. Clear mission, purpose, goals, and strategic plan
  2. Proper financial systems to track and report grant funds
  3. Adequate staff or ability to hire with grant funds
  4. Leadership ready to meet grant requirements (reports, evaluations, events)
  5. Technology access, website, and social media presence

Ways to Seek Funds

Fundraising Strategies
  • Grantseeking
  • Email fundraising
  • Social media strategies
  • Text/Mobile strategies
  • Individual major giving
Funding Sources (Most to Least Likely)
  1. Individuals
  2. Foundations
  3. Bequests (inheritance)
  4. Corporations

Categories of Support

  • Operating: General organizational expenses
  • Program: Specific project/program costs
  • Capacity Building: Organizational development
  • Capital/Equipment: Physical assets and infrastructure
  • Research: Studies and investigations

Types of Proposals

Concept Paper

A brief summary (2-3 pages) providing an overview of the project to the funder. Focuses on alignment with funder's priorities, describes the need, and outlines the plan to address it.

Letter Proposal

A 3-4 page document sometimes requested by corporations. Describes the project plan, introduces the organization, and includes the specific funding request.

Full Proposal

Most commonly requested by foundations. Includes cover letter, proposal summary, and detailed information about the project and community significance. May require various attachments and is often submitted through online portals.

💡 Step 1: Developing the Proposal Idea

What Makes a Project "Fundable"?

✅ Preferable
  • New Project
  • Expanding Project
❌ Non-Preferable
  • General Operating Support
  • Program Continuation

Matching Project Ideas to Funders

Conduct Prospect Research on funders to identify:

  • Programmatic priorities (objectives & goals)
  • Geographic focus
  • Issue areas of interest
  • Funding restrictions and requirements

Things to Consider Before Starting

Involving the Right People Having appropriate staff in proposal planning is crucial to avoid funding programs that cannot be implemented or don't meet identified needs.
Understanding Proposal Structure Start with planning sections (problem statement, goals, objectives, strategies, evaluation, sustainability, budget) as the core. Background, summary, and cover letter follow.
Time Allocation Nonprofits should dedicate approximately 70% of time to program planning and 30% to organizational background, summary, and submission.

Six Key Proposal Development Questions

  1. What community need does the program address? → Need Statement
  2. What would improvement look like? → Goals & Objectives
  3. What can the organization do to improve the situation? → Strategies
  4. How will we know if it succeeded? → Evaluation
  5. How much will it cost and what other support exists? → Budget
  6. How will it be funded in the future? → Sustainability

🤝 Step 2: Developing Relationships with Funders

What is Prospect Research?

The process of gathering information and evaluating potential sources of funding or support for a project. It involves researching and identifying individuals, organizations, or institutions interested in providing resources.

Information to Gather from Potential Funders

  • Grantmaker's funding interests and priorities
  • Grantmaking process and timeline
  • Board and staff members
  • Other relevant details (past grants, funding amounts, restrictions)

Where to Find This Information

🌐 Online (Website) Review funder websites, annual reports, grant databases, and IRS 990 forms
👥 Approach Directly Contact funders through email, phone, or meetings to learn about priorities

Ways to Develop Relationships

1. Sending Email Inquiries

Think of it as an introduction Keep it brief! Don't write a mini-proposal. Introduce your organization and express interest in their funding priorities.

2. Contacting by Telephone

  • Keep conversation short - they might not have time
  • Goal is to make a connection
  • Determine if there are follow-up steps

3. Funder Meetings

Topics to cover:

  • Credibility of the organization
  • Need for the proposed project
  • Program description and community interest
  • Proposed outcomes and ability to measure success
  • Costs and projected revenue sources
  • Why funder's interests align with your program

4. Writing a Letter of Inquiry (LOI)

A "sneak peek" of your organization
  • Set target audience and prospective program
  • Not a full proposal
  • Tests funder interest before full submission

Understanding Public Funders

Building relationships with public funders is different as they are government entities:

  • NOFA: Notice of Funding Availability
  • RFA: Request for Applications
  • Prebid Conference/Webinar: Information sessions before submission
  • General Information Sessions: Learn about funding opportunities

🎯 Step 3: Writing a Compelling Problem Statement

What is a Problem Statement?

A concise and focused description of a specific issue or condition that your organization aims to address through a proposed project or intervention.

Why Do We Need a Problem Statement?

  • Informs readers/funders about problems the project addresses
  • Sets the framework for the entire proposal
  • Highlights beneficiaries, locations, timeframe, and necessary activities
  • Creates agreement between funders and grantseekers on issue importance

Content of Problem Statement

Relation to Mission and Purpose

The problem must directly connect to your organization's mission. Show how addressing this issue aligns with your core purpose.

Focus on Community Needs

Center the problem on those who will be served, not on your organization. Describe the community need, not organizational need.

Support Assertions with Evidence

Use solid quantitative and qualitative data. Include statistics, research findings, and credible sources to validate the problem.

Connect Need to Your Ability

Explain how your organization, given its size and resources, can meaningfully address the problem.

Make It Easily Digestible

KISS - Keep It Sweet and Simple. Write clearly and concisely so readers quickly understand the issue.

Do Not Overpromise

Be realistic about what can be achieved. Avoid claiming to solve problems beyond your scope or capacity.

Tips for Writing a Good Problem Statement

  1. Use Clear and Relevant Statistics: Choose data that directly supports your case
  2. Utilize Comparative Statistics: Show how your community compares to others
  3. Quote Credible Authorities: Reference experts, studies, and reputable sources
  4. Document Data Sources: Always cite where your information comes from
  5. Use Compelling Stories: Include anecdotes that humanize the problem
  6. Convey a Sense of Urgency: Explain why this problem needs addressing now

Review Questions

  • Is the problem statement focused on those served (not the organization)?
  • Does it directly connect to the organization's mission?
  • Does it explain how the organization can meaningfully address the problem?
  • Is it supported by solid quantitative and qualitative data?
  • Is it KISS (Keep It Sweet and Simple)?

🎯 Step 4: Defining Clear Goals and Objectives

Definitions & Differences

Mission

The ongoing reason for your project or organization's existence.

Example: Empower individuals through education, fostering lifelong learning, and building a literate community.

Goal

The vision the program wants to achieve by solving specific problems. May not be measurable.

Example: Increase literacy rates and foster lifelong learning through training programs to enhance literacy skills among teens.

Objective

The results or changes expected after completion of work. Objectives are measurable and time-bound.

Example: Increase literacy rates and foster lifelong learning by 10% within six months through training programs to enhance literacy skills among teens of ABC community.

Related Concepts

  • Strategies: The "how to" - activities or methods to accomplish objectives
  • Activity: The work undertaken to achieve goals
  • Outcome: What will have changed as a result of your work (objectives/accomplishments)
  • Output: Materials or products created (posters, videos, workshops)
  • Benchmarks: How you'll know if objectives are achieved (measures of success)

Types of Objectives

Output Objectives

Focus on deliverables or tangible results - specific products, services, or outcomes to deliver.

Example: Create an interactive and user-friendly website that fosters a sense of community among Cambodian educators, especially Gen Z.

Outcome Objectives

Focus on desired impacts or changes (knowledge, awareness, increase/decrease) the project aims to achieve.

Example: Raise awareness and knowledge of inclusive education among Cambodian educators, especially Gen Z educators, through social media campaign within 3 months.

Process Objectives

Concerned with activities, tasks, or procedures needed for effective project implementation.

Example: Establish partnerships with Cambodian educators, especially Gen Z, to develop and produce an educational podcast series on inclusive teaching practices.

How to Write Objectives (WHAT, WHO, HOW, WHEN)

  1. WHAT: Based on the problem statement, what key area(s) is the organization seeking to change?
  2. WHO/WHERE: Who (what segment of the population and where located) will be involved in the change?
  3. HOW: How will change be measured (increase/improvement or decrease/reduction)? By what degree?
  4. WHEN: When will this change take place? How many months/years or by what date?

SMART Objectives Framework

S - Specific: Answers WHAT, WHO, WHERE, HOW, and WHEN
M - Measurable: Can determine if goal is met; has benchmarks
A - Achievable: Based on available resources (funding, staffing, partners, space)
R - Relevant: Results-oriented and rewarding to organization, funders, and community
T - Time-Bound: Has a clear deadline

Objective Formulas

1. Outcome Objective Formula

[Action Verb] + [Key Result/Change] + of [Theme/Topic] + to/among [Number] + [Target Audience + Location] + via/through [Method/Action] + by/within [Deadline]

Example: To raise awareness of menstrual health and hygiene among 35 female students aged 13-16 at Samdach Preah Mahasagharachja Bour Kry Secondary School, Kandal Province, through a Telegram group by May.

2. Output Objective Formula

[Action Verb] + [Number] + [Deliverable/Product/Action] + for/to + [Number] + [Target Audience + Location] + by/within [Deadline]

Example: Donating 10 packages of sanitary pads to 35 female students aged 13-16 at Samdach Preah Mahasagharachja Bour Kry Secondary School in Kandal Province by June.

[Action Verb] + [Number] + [Deliverable/Product/Action] + on [Theme/Topic] + for/to + [Number] + [Target Audience + Location] + by/within [Deadline]

Example: To conduct a workshop on climate change-related harms and prevention tips for approximately 20 student participants from the Department of English, Institute of Foreign Languages by June.

📋 Step 5: Developing the Strategies

What are Strategies?

Strategies (also known as workplan) tell funders the steps the organization proposes to carry out to accomplish its objectives. Strategies answer: "How will an organization actually accomplish the work for which it seeks funding?"

Content of the Strategies Component

Definition Detailed descriptions of activities the organization will conduct to achieve the ends specified in objectives
Research Has this approach been tested? Is there data showing success?
Status of Plan Is this being used, replicated, or is it new/innovative?
Work Division Who will work on what? Clear assignment of responsibilities

Questions to Answer When Developing Strategies

  1. What activities need to be carried out to meet the objectives?
  2. What are the starting and ending dates of these activities?
  3. Who has responsibility for completing each activity?
  4. How will those served be identified or participants selected?
  5. How was this methodology determined to be the best? Does it build on existing models or is it different? Why was it selected?
⚠️ REMINDER It is important to develop your objectives BEFORE developing your strategies. Do NOT construct your objectives around your strategies.

Strategies Review Questions

  • Do strategies derive logically from problem statement, goals, and objectives?
  • Do they present program activities to be undertaken?
  • Has the grantseeker explained why specific strategies were selected?
  • Is the timing and order of activities clear?
  • Is it clear who will perform specific activities? Do their credentials demonstrate appropriateness?
  • Given projected resources, are proposed activities feasible?

📊 Step 6: Preparing the Evaluation Component

What is Evaluation?

A process that determines the impact, effectiveness, and efficiency of a program. It helps identify the effectiveness of your work and plan for the program's future.

Purpose of Evaluation

Return on Investment (ROI)

The amount of benefit (return) based on the resources (funds, hours of work) used to produce it.

  • Financial Returns: Revenue, cost savings
  • Non-Financial Returns: Social impact, community benefit, knowledge gained

The Virtues of Evaluation

🛡️ Reassuring Funders Shows accountability and proves grant money is well-spent
📚 Learning and Improving Identifies what works and areas for improvement
✅ Ensuring Positive Impact Confirms the program is making the desired difference
📈 Evidence for Future Funding Provides data to support future grant applications

Internal vs External Evaluation

Internal Evaluation
  • Conducted by organization staff
  • Less expensive
  • Deep organizational knowledge
  • May lack objectivity
External Evaluation
  • Conducted by outside evaluators
  • More objective and credible
  • Brings fresh perspective
  • Higher cost

Types of Evaluation Data

Quantitative Data

Numerical data that can be measured and analyzed statistically.

Examples: Number of participants, percentage increases, test scores, attendance rates, cost per participant

Qualitative Data

Descriptive data that captures experiences, perceptions, and stories.

Examples: Interviews, focus groups, observations, case studies, participant testimonials

Evaluation Review Questions

  1. Does the evaluation focus on assessing project results?
  2. Does it explain how evaluation will assess efficiency of program methods?
  3. Does it describe who will be evaluated and what will be measured?
  4. Does it specify what information will be collected?
  5. Does it state who will be responsible for making assessments?
  6. Does it discuss how information and conclusions will be used to improve the program?
  7. Does it provide the organization's definition of success?

🌱 Step 7: Developing a Statement of Sustainability

What is Sustainability?

A section within a proposal that outlines how you or your organization plans to maintain operations and resources over the long term. It includes strategies for securing ongoing funding, diversifying revenue, and leveraging partnerships.

Why Consider Sustainability?

  • Funders want to know programs will continue after their support ends
  • Demonstrates long-term planning and organizational viability
  • Shows commitment beyond one-time funding
  • Proves impact will be lasting, not temporary

Content by Grant Type

General Operating Grants

Outline diverse revenue streams that support ongoing operations:

  • Individual donor base development
  • Earned income strategies
  • Multiple foundation/corporate partnerships
  • Endowment building
Project-Specific Grants

Explain how the program will continue after grant period:

  • Integration into operating budget
  • New funding sources identified
  • Earned income from project
  • Community support and partnerships
Capital or Equipment Grants

Show how assets will be maintained and utilized:

  • Maintenance budget allocation
  • Operating costs coverage
  • Long-term use plans
Capacity-Building Grants

Demonstrate how new capacity will be sustained:

  • Staff retention plans
  • Ongoing training investments
  • System maintenance

Sustainability Strategy Sources

Grants from Others Foundations and corporations beyond initial funder
Individual Donors Building a base of recurring individual supporters
Fees for Service Charging for services provided
Sales of Items/Activities Revenue from products or events

Sustainability Review Questions

  1. Is it the organization's intent to continue the program after initial grant funding ends?
  2. If yes, does the sustainability component present a plan for securing future funding?
  3. Does it discuss future funding strategies or earned-income strategies?
  4. If requesting multiyear grant, does it show decreasing reliance on grant support each year?
💡 Note for Multiyear Grants Grantmakers prefer to see decreasing reliance on their support each year, with the organization assuming greater financial responsibility for the project.

💰 Step 8: Developing the Proposal Budget

What is a Budget Plan?

A detailed financial plan that outlines estimated income and expenses for a specific period. It's a systematic approach to allocating resources and managing financial aspects of a program or organization.

Types of Budget Plans

1. General Support (GS) Budget

Outlines income and expenses for a fiscal year or grant period, aligned with organizational goals and values.

Basic Guidelines:
  • Organize income and expenses into clear categories
  • Include all staff costs (salaries, benefits, taxes)
  • Directors and Officers liability insurance is important
  • Things add up [Indirect costs] - administrative overhead, utilities, rent
  • Create a modest cushion for unexpected expenses

2. Program Budget

Focuses on costs associated with a specific body of work or program.

Stages of Budget Development

1. Review, Research, and Organize Gather cost information, review organizational needs, research market rates
2. Develop the Budget Create line items, calculate costs, ensure alignment with proposal
3. Review, Update, and Review Again Check calculations, get feedback, ensure accuracy
4. Develop the Budget Narrative Explain each line item, justify costs, provide context

Common Budget Categories

Personnel Costs

  • Salaries and wages
  • Benefits (health insurance, retirement)
  • Payroll taxes
  • Consultant fees

Non-Personnel Costs

  • Rent and utilities
  • Supplies and materials
  • Equipment and technology
  • Travel and transportation
  • Marketing and communications
  • Program-specific expenses

Indirect Costs (Overhead)

  • Administrative salaries
  • Office rent and utilities
  • Accounting and legal services
  • Insurance
Budget Narrative

Explains and justifies each budget line item. It should:

  • Describe how costs were calculated
  • Explain why items are necessary
  • Show how costs align with strategies
  • Demonstrate cost-effectiveness

🏢 Step 9: Writing the Organizational Background

Purpose of Organizational Background

Provides information about WHO is proposing the project. Gives funders confidence in the organization's ability to achieve its goals.

Key Objectives

  • Provide information about the nonprofit's mission, values, and distinguishing characteristics
  • Highlight why the organization is capable of delivering on its promises
  • Build funder confidence in organizational capacity

Key Points to Include

History

Founding story, when and why the organization was created, major milestones and achievements

Community Demographics

Who the organization serves, geographic area covered, population characteristics and needs

Innovative Programs & Special Services

Unique approaches, special initiatives, programs that set the organization apart

Credibility & Success

Track record of success, awards and recognition, partnerships and collaborations, testimonials and impact stories

Tips for Writing Organizational Background

  1. Start with the organization's founding story and mission statement
  2. Explain what the organization does, how it operates, and whom it serves
  3. Tailor the background for each funder by highlighting relevant information
  4. Focus on key information that strengthens the case for support
  5. Avoid unnecessary details that don't add value

For New Organizations or Youth Teams

If the organization is new without a long history:

Mission & Vision Clearly state your purpose and aspirations
Founding Story Explain what inspired creation of the organization/team
Experiences & Expertise Highlight relevant skills and background of team members
Future Plans (Optional) Show vision for growth and sustainability
💡 Remember Even without extensive history, new organizations can demonstrate capability through team expertise, passion, community connections, and thoughtful planning.

📝 Step 10: Writing the Proposal Summary

What is a Proposal Summary?

A concise and clear abstract of the entire proposal. It should highlight the strongest aspects to make the funder want to learn more or read the full proposal. The summary typically serves as the opening element.

Other Names for Proposal Summary

Project Synopsis Project Abstract Project Overview Executive Summary

Main Components

The summary should be concise (no more than 1-2 pages) and include:

  1. Identify your organization - Brief introduction
  2. State the purpose of the project/grant - What you aim to accomplish
  3. Highlight organization's qualifications - Why you're capable
  4. Describe anticipated results - Expected outcomes and impact
  5. Provide the total budget - Amount requested
  6. State the duration - Project timeframe

Tips for Writing the Summary

Decide on Key Points Identify the most important point from each section of the proposal. Include only those points in the summary.
Stress Points Important to Funder Make sure the summary highlights the potential funder's priorities and interests.
REMEMBER The summary should provide a clear understanding of the need for your project, its goals, and objectives. It should be well-written and engaging to set your proposal apart from others.

Why is the Proposal Summary Important?

  • First Impression: Often the first (sometimes only) part funders read
  • Decision Factor: Can determine if funder reads the full proposal
  • Quick Overview: Busy reviewers can quickly understand your project
  • Standalone Document: May be circulated separately for initial screening
⚠️ Writing Order Although the summary appears first in the proposal, write it LAST after completing all other sections. This ensures you can accurately summarize the full proposal.

📤 Step 11: Submitting the Application

Submission Methods

Hard Copy Submission Physical documents mailed or delivered in person
Soft Copy Submission Electronic submission via email or online portals

Writing a Cover Letter

For hard copy submissions, always include a cover letter unless explicitly asked not to.

Cover Letter Should Include:

  1. Brief Organization Introduction
    • Introduce your organization or team
    • State the requested amount
    • Explain the purpose of the grant
  2. Proposal Highlights
    • Concise overview of the proposal
    • Key objectives
    • Important points that align with funder priorities
  3. Appreciation & Contact Info
    • Thank the funder for consideration
    • Provide contact information for inquiries
    • Invite further interaction
NOTE The letter should be signed by the executive director, board president, or both. If the contact person is different, clearly mention their name, title, email, and phone extension.

Writing a Cover Email

For email submissions, write a short cover email similar to a cover letter but more concise.

Hello [Funder Name], On behalf of [Organization], I am pleased to submit the attached proposal for [Project Name] for your consideration. We are seeking [Amount] for [Purpose]. We have included the following attachments: [list all attachments]. Should you need anything further or have questions, please contact me at [phone] or [email]. We deeply appreciate your consideration and look forward to hearing from you. [Name] [Title]

Content of Appendices/Attachments

Common required documents:

  • Organization registration documents/licenses
  • Most recent audited financial statement
  • List of board members with work affiliations
  • CVs of main staff working on the project
  • Overall budget for current fiscal year
  • Latest annual report (if applicable)
  • Letter of inquiry and/or letter proposal (if applicable)

Online Portal Submissions

TIPS for Online Portals:

  • Check if account setup is needed in the portal
  • Confirm registration number requirements
  • Ensure the portal allows saving of submissions in progress
  • Submit before the deadline to avoid portal crashes

Common Portal Types:

Email Application Online Form Dedicated Website

Email vs Paper Submission Guidelines

Emailing the Proposal
  • Convert to PDF format
  • Verify recipient names and emails
  • Follow subject line requirements
  • List all included attachments
  • Provide contact email
  • Double-check CC line
Paper Proposal
  • Read funder's guidelines for number of copies
  • Submit correct number of copies
  • Ensure proper binding if required
  • Include cover letter
  • Organize attachments clearly
  • Mail with tracking

🤝 Step 12: Sustaining Relationships with Funders

After Submission: What's Next?

After submitting your proposal, it's essential to follow up and maintain relationships with funders.

1. Following Up on the Proposal

Pay Attention to Guidelines and Deadlines Review the funder's timeline for decisions and respect their process.
Respect Funder Requests If they ask not to call or email during specific periods, honor that request.
Be Ready for Site Visits Some funders conduct site visits as part of their review process. Be prepared!

2. Managing a Site Visit

A site visit is when the funder visits your organization or proposed program location.

Preparation Steps:

  1. Confirm participation of key program staff
  2. Share the full proposal and ask everyone to review it
  3. Distribute any advance questions from the funder
  4. Hold a meeting to align everyone's knowledge and roles
  5. Plan and schedule a tour if requested
  6. Ensure the tour showcases important program elements
  7. Coordinate logistics and inform all participants
  8. Check with staff to avoid disruptions or privacy issues

3. Responding to the Funder's Decision

Regardless of the result, continue building relationships with funders.

When the Proposal is Funded ✅

  • Send a formal thank-you letter signed by the executive director
  • Consider recognizing the funder in newsletters or donor lists
  • Maintain regular communication and provide program updates
  • Submit required reports on time
  • Invite funder to events or program activities
  • Share success stories and impact data

When the Proposal is Not Funded ❌

Remember: Proposals may be declined due to high competition for limited funds, not necessarily because of project quality.
  • Gather Feedback: Ask funders for feedback to improve future applications
  • Keep Funders Informed: Continue sharing organizational news and updates
  • Build Relationships: Maintain contact for future opportunities
  • Thank Them: Express appreciation for their time and consideration
  • Ask About Resubmission: Inquire if you can apply again in the future

Final Thoughts

Relationship Building is Ongoing

Whether funded or not, maintaining positive relationships with funders creates opportunities for future support. Funders appreciate organizations that are professional, communicative, and mission-focused.

✍️ Practice Application Question

Scenario: Reducing Plastic Waste at RUPP

Context:

In Phnom Penh, citizens use over 2,000 plastic bags per year on average. At RUPP, thousands of daily discarded items include plastic bags, bottles, takeaway containers, and disposable coffee cups. You have been allocated $5,000 USD to implement a pilot project between 2025-2026.

Key Issues Identified:

  1. Low Awareness of Water Dispensers: Limited knowledge of existing water refill stations; perception of unclean water; inconvenience of carrying reusable bottles
  2. Habit of Using Single-Use Plastics: Convenience and routine; limited alternatives for food and beverage purchases

What You Need to Develop:

  1. Problem Statement (150 words)
    • Identify a specific issue
    • Provide clear justification
    • Use paraphrased information with proper sources
  2. One Goal and Two SMART Objectives
    • Must effectively highlight the project
    • Follow SMART criteria
  3. Two Activities per Objective (100 words each)
    • Clear justification and reasons
  4. Evaluation Plan (100 words)
    • Based on your activities and plan
    • Clear justification
💡 Study Tip Practice writing this complete proposal following all the steps you've learned. This type of comprehensive question tests your understanding of the entire grantseeking process.
